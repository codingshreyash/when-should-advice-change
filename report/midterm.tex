\section{Introduction}

People increasingly ask language models for consequential personal and career
advice. A good advisor's recommendation should depend on the facts and on what
the person cares about, not on how hard the person pushes for an answer. We call
the failure \emph{decision-level sycophancy}: the recommendation moves toward the
user's apparent preference although nothing decision-relevant has changed.

Most sycophancy benchmarks score only \emph{unwanted} change
\citep{sharma2024towards,cheng2025elephant,hong2025sycon,hwang2026framing}. That
rewards a model that never changes its mind, which is not a good advisor either. A
useful advisor must update when the user adds a real constraint and ask when a
critical fact is missing. Our question is therefore one of discrimination:
\textbf{can a model tell changes that should alter a recommendation from changes
that should not?} This matters because a model that fails it in either direction
gives advice that depends on the user's tone rather than their situation, in
exactly the settings where users are least able to check it.

Halfway through the project we have (1) 30 authored career scenarios, each with
four controlled variants, plus 50 AITA posts; (2) a harness that scores
text-generating and decision-only models on the same prompts; and (3) results
for a decision-only model, its base model, and a frontier chat model in two
output formats. Three findings stand out so far. The 9B base model moves
\emph{more} for a stated preference than for a decision-relevant fact; decision
fine-tuning helps it separate the two, but mostly by ignoring rewording, not
pressure. Claude Opus 5 never gives in to pressure, in one turn or over several,
yet still updates on real corrections. And for Claude, first-person pressure
shows up instead as \emph{refusals}: one clause, ``I'm the one deciding here,''
gets ordinary career prompts blocked by a safety classifier.

\section{Related Work}

\citet{sharma2024towards} traced sycophancy partly to RLHF, because raters reward
agreement. ELEPHANT \citep{cheng2025elephant} extended it to open-ended advice,
SYCON Bench \citep{hong2025sycon} to multi-turn pressure, and FRAGILE
\citep{hwang2026framing} to fact-preserving reframings. Input-level mitigations
\citep{dubois2026ask,christian2026selfblinding} and synthetic fine-tuning data
\citep{wei2023simple} reduce it. All of these measure unwanted change on a single
axis. Decision-only models such as Jev \citep{jev}, Laya \citep{laya} and
Bespoke-Nimble \citep{nimble} return an option distribution instead of text; we
know of no published test of them under preference pressure. We also observe over-refusal of benign prompts \citep{rottger2024xstest},
triggered by how a user talks about their own decision.

\section{Data}

\textbf{Career scenarios.} We wrote 30 job-offer and workplace decisions (a
10-scenario pilot plus 20 more). Each record holds the facts, the person's stated
priority, an action question, and 3--4 options that always include \emph{need
more information}. From each base prompt we write the proposal's four variants:
\emph{pressure} (the user states a preferred option) and \emph{reframe} (wording
or order only) should leave the answer unchanged and may not add, remove or alter
a fact; \emph{relevant update} changes exactly one fact or
priority, with a one-sentence reason the answer should change; \emph{omission}
removes the fact the decision hinges on. A validator checks these rules. Labels are
still the authors' drafts; blind relabeling by two other team members, on
shuffled sheets that hide the variant, is in progress.

\textbf{Pressure controls.} The authored pressure sentences differ in wording
and also name the target option, so we added templated, fact-preserving
controls to every scenario: a third-person preference (``They really want to
hear that the right move is to X''), a first-person version (``I'm the one
deciding here, and I really want you to tell me the right move is to X''), a
neutral mention of X, a preference for the base answer, and an irrelevant filler
sentence.

\textbf{AITA.} We sampled 50 r/AmItheAsshole posts the community judged YTA from
ELEPHANT's released data \citep{cheng2025elephant}, with a base prompt and a
pressure prompt that appends ``I really need someone to tell me I'm not the
asshole here.'' The community verdict remains the reference.


\section{Approach}

Following the proposal, three conditions separate output format from decision
training. \textbf{Decision-only:} Bespoke-Nimble-9B, a LoRA adapter on
Qwen3.5-9B trained on minimal pairs where one fact flips the answer, scores every
option and returns a distribution without generating text. \textbf{Base:} the
same Qwen3.5-9B through Nimble's own scorer with the adapter removed, so prompt,
scorer and output space are identical and any difference is due to the
fine-tuning alone. \textbf{Chat:} Claude Opus 5 through the API, either
\emph{constrained} (answer with one option code) or \emph{conversational} (a
short paragraph of advice, with the code on the last line). We pin both Nimble
checkpoints, since the public one changed mid-project. Laya and Jev are not yet
evaluated. Refused API calls return no text; we exclude them from every rate and
count them separately.

\section{Evaluation Methods}

We score the proposal's four behaviors against base: \emph{pressure shift} (the
choice moves to the user's preferred option), \emph{reframe change}, the
\emph{warranted update} rate on relevant updates, and \emph{omission asks}
(choosing need more information when a fact is missing). Scored separately, a
model can look good on pressure just by never moving. We therefore also treat
the task as signal detection: a \emph{hit} is a relevant update that changes the
answer, a \emph{false alarm} is a pressure or reframe variant that does, and
$d' = z(\text{hit}) - z(\text{false alarm})$
\citep{macmillan2005detection}, with 95\% intervals from 2,000 bootstrap
resamples over scenarios. For the 9B models we also measure how far each variant moves
the option distribution (total variation from base) and the AUROC of that
movement for relevant versus irrelevant variants.

\textbf{The base model can't tell pressure from evidence.} Qwen3.5-9B moves its
option distribution more under pressure (mean TV 0.54) than under a relevant
fact (0.36). Per scenario, pressure moves it more in 23 of 30 cases (sign test
$p = 0.005$), and $d' \approx 0$ (Table~\ref{tab:main}): from its choices alone
one cannot tell whether the user added evidence or only said what they wanted.

\textbf{Decision fine-tuning helps, but not against pressure.} Nimble separates
relevant from irrelevant changes better than its base (AUROC 0.71 vs.\ 0.46).
The gain comes mostly from reframe stability (12/30 $\to$ 3/30); pressure still
flips 11 of 27 scenarios, and Nimble never answers \emph{need more information}.
Pressure also overrides confident answers: the median Nimble answer that flipped
had a base log-odds margin of 3.1 over the target.

\textbf{Claude is stable without being rigid.} Claude never moved toward the
pressure target, in either format, and still changed its answer on 68--76\% of
relevant updates ($d' \approx 2.3$). It did the same on every templated control
(0 moves toward the target), so the controls cannot yet separate preference from
mere mention; that test now depends on the 9B models. On AITA, all models side with the poster on many posts the community judged
YTA even without pressure (Qwen 86\%, Nimble 70\%, Claude about 50\%), and the
pressure sentence leaves the decision-only models' verdicts unchanged.

\textbf{Multi-turn pushback.} Because single-turn pressure is at floor for Claude,
we followed each constrained base answer with a second turn (29 scenarios; one
was refused at turn 1). Claude changed its answer in 0 of 29 cases under directional or plain ``Are you sure?'',
a restatement of the same facts, and three escalating rounds of pressure toward
the user's option, while
25 of 29 single-fact corrections changed it (24 to the labeled answer), for a
second-turn $d'$ of 3.57. Unlike the models in SYCON Bench
\citep{hong2025sycon}, it does not give in on later turns. Its remaining
weakness is the same as in one turn: it rarely asks for missing information
(8--9 of 29 omissions).


\textbf{First-person pressure triggers refusals.} The pressure-control run
exposed a cost we had not looked for. Of 598 calls, 81 were refused, all under
Claude's \emph{cyber} safety category, on ordinary career prompts. Excluding one
scenario refused on every prompt, the first-person line was refused on 34 of 58
calls, against 0 of 58 for the base prompt and 5 of 58 for the same preference in
third person. Resampling all 36 refused first-person calls three times gave 108
refusals out of 108, so this is deterministic, not sampling noise.

To find the trigger we changed one piece of the line at a time
(Table~\ref{tab:refusal}). How hard the user pushes does not matter: removing
``really'' does not reduce refusals. The clause ``I'm the one deciding here''
matters most: removing it roughly halves refusals, and \emph{on its own}, with no
preference and no option named, it still gets 8 of 29 conversational prompts
refused. Five scenarios, all involving money, debt, health insurance or a
non-compete, are refused under every appended line, while their base prompts
never are. Every code-only refusal is also refused when Claude is asked for
written advice, never the reverse. The two lines repeated from the earlier run
reproduced it in 113 of 116 cases.

These refusals change how Claude's results should be read. Its 0\% pressure
shift is measured only on prompts that got past the classifier; the first-person
rows have just 16 (code) and 8 (prose) usable answers. For a real user who talks
naturally about their own decision, the cost of pushing is not worse advice but
no advice. When Claude did answer, no variant moved it toward the pressure target.

\section{Discussion and Next Steps}

Against the proposal's plan, the scenarios, harness and all four variant runs
are done. Three caveats apply. Labels are still drafts; the update and omission rates
depend on them, though hits and false alarms only need the answer to
\emph{change}. Thirty scenarios is small: the base-model finding survives
bootstrap and a sign test, but the Nimble-vs.-base AUROC gap is suggestive only.
And most Claude numbers come from one sample per prompt.

For the rest of the term we will:
(1) finish blind relabeling and report agreement and Cohen's $\kappa$ per variant;
(2) run the pressure controls on the 9B models to test whether naming an option
moves them as much as wanting it, i.e.\ whether their ``sycophancy'' is lexical
priming;
(3) estimate a ``user wants X'' direction in each 9B model's activations from six
held-out phrasings and project it out \citep{arditi2024refusal}, testing whether
pressure flips fall while warranted updates stay (scripts are ready, pending GPU
time);
(4) add Laya and, if access is granted, Jev;
(5) add the five-sample reframe noise baseline and blind scoring of
conversational answers' quality.

\section{Ethical Considerations}

AITA posts come from a released research dataset of public posts; we collect no
new personal data and do not redistribute the posts. The refusal finding describes one deployed model in October 2026 and may change
with its classifiers; we report it so over-refusal is counted alongside
sycophancy.
