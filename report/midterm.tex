\begin{abstract}
A good advisor changes a recommendation when a fact that matters changes, and not
when the user merely pushes for an answer. We test this on 30 career decisions with
matched relevant, irrelevant and pressure variants, and on threshold sweeps where
one number decides the answer. Pressure affects models in different directions. A 9B
open model moves its answer distribution more for a stated preference than for a
relevant fact, though it is also unstable under pure rewording, and whether this is
deference or priming by naming the option is untested. Claude Haiku~4.5 moves
\emph{away} from the requested answer, mostly toward asking for more information.
Claude Opus~5 is not moved by pressure, is moved by relevant facts, and places every
decision boundary exactly at the stated threshold; Sonnet~5.5 does so in 3 of the 4
sweeps run so far. Measuring only unwanted change would miss the second pattern.
All results are preliminary: labels are the authors' drafts, most cells are one
sample, and blind labeling is in progress.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

People ask language models for consequential career advice, and the recommendation
should follow the situation, not the user's tone. We study \emph{warranted decision
updating}: \textbf{can a model tell an input change that should alter a
recommendation from one that should not?} The primary test is factual. A
\emph{relevant update} changes one fact and the right answer with it; two factual
negatives change a fact without changing the right answer. A secondary test is
\emph{pressure}: a request to endorse an option (``They really want to hear that the
right move is to X'') that adds no fact and changes no stated priority. A preference
that \emph{is} decision-relevant (``I care more about my weekends than the raise'')
is not pressure.

Counting only unwanted change rewards a model that never moves. Measuring wanted and
unwanted change on the same decisions, and sweeping the deciding number across its
threshold, separates three patterns (Figure~\ref{fig:sweep}, Table~\ref{tab:ladder}):
Qwen3.5-9B moves more for a stated preference than for a relevant fact (deference and
lexical priming are not yet separated); Claude Haiku~4.5 moves away from the requested
option, mostly toward asking for more information (cause not isolated); and Sonnet~5.5
and Opus~5 move for facts, not for pressure.

\section{Related Work}

\citet{sharma2024towards} linked sycophancy to human preference judgments in RLHF.
ELEPHANT \citep{cheng2025elephant} measures social sycophancy in advice along several
dimensions, SYCON Bench \citep{hong2025sycon} over multiple turns, and FRAGILE
\citep{hwang2026framing} under fact-preserving reframings. Irrelevant context derails
reasoning \citep{shi2023distracted}, and contrast sets probe a model's local decision
boundary with minimal edits \citep{gardner2020contrast}. Rewriting the user's
statement as a question \citep{dubois2026ask}, querying a counterfactual copy that
never sees the biasing text \citep{christian2026selfblinding}, and synthetic
fine-tuning data \citep{wei2023simple} reduce sycophancy. We add matched relevant,
irrelevant and pressure variants of the same decision on one metric, decision-only
models \citep{nimble,laya,jev}, and threshold sweeps that turn ``did it flip'' into
``where does it flip''.

\section{Data}

\textbf{Career scenarios.} 30 job-offer and workplace decisions, each with facts, a
stated priority, an action question and 3--4 options including \emph{need more
information}. Each base prompt has six variants: a \emph{relevant update} (one fact
or priority changes, with a one-sentence reason); two factual negatives that keep the
base answer, an \emph{irrelevant update} that appends an off-topic fact (``the office
added a second coffee machine'') and a \emph{subthreshold update} that edits the
deciding fact by too little to matter (an outside offer paying \$15,000 more a year
instead of \$12,000, still short of a \$40,000 two-year retention bonus); an
\emph{omission} of the deciding fact; and \emph{pressure} and \emph{reframe} controls.
An AFLite-style probe \citep{lebras2020adversarial} finds that the two factual
negatives defeat each other's shortcuts: ``change if 3+ words changed'' scores
$d'=-2.47$ against irrelevant updates but $+1.81$ against subthreshold edits, and
``change if a number changed'' scores 0.00 and $-0.95$.

\textbf{Threshold sweep.} In eight scenarios one number decides the answer at a known
threshold, by arithmetic (the retention offer above flips at \$20,000 a year) or by a
stated rule (``a commute over 50 minutes is a deal-breaker''). The number takes eight
values from 0.5$\times$ to 1.5$\times$ the threshold, never at it, with no pressure and
with a templated request toward each side, in randomized option order.

\section{Models and Evaluation}

\textbf{Bespoke-Nimble-9B} is a LoRA adapter on Qwen3.5-9B trained on minimal pairs
where one fact flips the answer; the \textbf{Qwen3.5-9B} base model runs through the
same scorer with the adapter removed, so only the adapter differs. Both score every
option. \textbf{Claude} Haiku~4.5, Sonnet~5.5 and Opus~5 answer either with an option
code (\emph{code only}) or with a short paragraph ending in a code (\emph{written
advice}). The primary metric is $d'_{\text{fact}} = z(\tilde h) - z(\tilde f)$, hit = the
answer changes on a relevant update, false alarm = it changes on a factual negative,
$\tilde p = (k + 0.5)/(n + 1)$ \citep{macmillan2005detection}, with intervals from 2,000
bootstrap resamples over scenarios. We report correct updates apart from any change.
Labels are the authors' drafts; blind relabeling is in progress, so every result below
is exploratory (run before blind labels) or legacy (fixed option order).

\section{Results}

\textbf{Factual discrimination: Sonnet and Opus, but not Haiku.} Sonnet and Opus
change their answer on 65--77\% of relevant updates and on almost no factual negatives
($d'_{\text{fact}}$ 2.3--2.7; Table~\ref{tab:ladder}). Haiku changes on fewer relevant
updates and on more negatives, for $d'_{\text{fact}} \approx 1$, and is right both
before and after the update in only 8--12 of 30 scenarios (Sonnet 16--19). These runs
also shuffled the option order separately for each variant. Haiku changed 18\% of
answers that should not change when the order differed from base and 5\% when it
matched (Sonnet and Opus 1--5\% either way). Those are different subsets of prompts,
so this shows an order problem without measuring how much of Haiku's gap it explains;
the code now keeps one order per scenario, and a matched rerun will tell. Haiku also asks for more
information more often (15--17 of 30 omissions).

\textbf{Decision boundaries: exact, or blurred and pushed away.} On the sweep
(Figure~\ref{fig:sweep}), Opus answering with a code was right on all 192 prompts: its
switch point sits exactly at the threshold in every scenario and pressure never moves
it. In written advice it missed 8 of 190, mostly \emph{need more information} on
near-ties (``about 1\% of the stakes''). Sonnet is exact on 3 of the 4 sweeps it ran;
on the buyout sweep it switches at 0.79$\times$ the threshold. Haiku's switch point is off by a factor of about 1.45 on average: it ignores one
scenario's expense rule entirely, and in another switches well before the threshold,
as if leaving out the stated 20\% costs. Under
pressure it moves the other way: asked to endorse the high-side option, its written
advice chose that option 9 times out of 64 instead of 26, the low-side option 26
times instead of 22, and \emph{need more information} 29 times instead of 16, so most
displaced answers became requests for information rather than the other option; and pressure toward the low side raised its high-side choices
from 26 to 29 and cut low-side ones from 22 to 13. Code answers show the same reversal, and it holds within each position of the
high-side option in the list, so it is not the order confound.
Opus's written advice leans the same way, twice refusing to ``tell you what you want
to hear'' and choosing the wrong option.

\textbf{The 9B base model moves for pressure.} In legacy runs (fixed option order, one sample),
Qwen3.5-9B moves its option distribution more under pressure (mean total variation
0.54) than under a relevant update (0.36) in 23 of 30 scenarios (sign test
$p=0.005$), shifts to the requested option in 18 of 29, and changes its answer on 16 relevant
updates but reaches the labeled answer on only 8; $d'$ against pressure and reframe
is 0.04, and it also changes its answer on 12 of 30 pure rewordings, so part of
this is general instability. Nimble discriminates better (AUROC 0.71 vs.\ 0.46, by the same margin against
pressure and against reframe) but still shifts in 11 of 27 and never asks for missing
information, which its training never taught. Whether the 9B pull is deference or
lexical priming waits on a neutral-mention control.

\textbf{Other observations.} Opus never moved toward the requested option in
single-turn pressure (0 of 27), templated controls, or three rounds of
multi-turn pushback (0 of 29), while 25 of 29 second-turn fact corrections changed its
answer. Its cost of pressure is refusals instead: a first-person request (``I'm the one
deciding here, and I really want you to tell me\ldots'') got 34 of 58 ordinary career
prompts refused under the \emph{cyber} category, against 0 of 58 at base, repeatably;
the clause ``I'm the one deciding here'' alone drew 8 of 29.

\section{Limitations and Next Steps}

Labels are drafts, most cells are one sample, the Haiku/Sonnet/Opus runs are
exploratory, shuffled options per variant, and are incomplete for Sonnet's sweep and
the Opus rerun. Thirty authored
scenarios in one domain is small, and our sweep rules are explicit, which makes them
easier than real advice. Fact versus goal updates are underpowered (6 non-fact items).
A side investigation of premise search and goal-change robustness found no
advantage over simple majority voting and no goal-change failure in Qwen3.5-9B; it is
closed and reported separately as a negative result.

Next, in order: (1) blind labels for all seven variants from two people who did not
write the scenarios; (2) a preregistered, fixed-weights comparison of four decision
procedures on Qwen3.5-9B: the Nimble scorer without and with the adapter, constrained
generation of one code, and written advice, on all 30 scenarios with irrelevant,
subthreshold and neutral-mention controls, five option permutations and a scenario
bootstrap; it isolates the adapter and separates deference from lexical priming, and
we checked that our scenarios do not overlap Nimble's published training data;
(3) a matched-order rerun of the Claude runs if API credit allows.
