\section{Introduction}

People increasingly ask language models for consequential personal and career
advice. Good advice should follow the facts and what the person values. It
should not follow how hard the person pushes for a particular answer. We study
\emph{decision-level sycophancy}: a recommendation moving toward the option the
user wants endorsed when nothing decision-relevant has changed.

That requires separating two things a user can express. A preference can be
decision-relevant (``I care more about my weekends than the raise''), and a good
advisor should weigh it. We define \emph{pressure} more narrowly: an expressed
desire for the advisor to endorse a specific option (``I really want you to tell
me the right move is X'') that adds no fact and does not change the person's
stated priorities. Only movement under pressure in this sense counts as
sycophancy. A useful advisor must also update when a relevant fact changes, and
ask when a critical fact is missing. Our question is whether models \textbf{tell
changes that should alter a recommendation from changes that should not}. A model
that fails in either direction gives advice that depends on the user's tone
rather than their situation.

At the midterm we have 30 career scenarios with matched variants, a harness that
scores text-generating and decision-only models on the same prompts, and results
for three setups. Our preliminary results are that models differ substantially in
this ability. Under identical scoring, Qwen3.5-9B is more sensitive to
preference-bearing text than to relevant facts. Decision fine-tuning improves its
discrimination, but sensitivity to pressure remains. Claude Opus 5 showed no
directional pressure shifts among the prompts it answered while responding to most
relevant updates, but that robustness has to be read alongside high,
wording-dependent refusal rates.

\section{Related Work}

\citet{sharma2024towards} linked sycophancy to human preference judgments used in
RLHF. ELEPHANT \citep{cheng2025elephant} measures social sycophancy in advice along
several dimensions (validation, moral endorsement, indirectness, accepting the
user's framing); SYCON Bench \citep{hong2025sycon} measures it over multiple
turns; and FRAGILE \citep{hwang2026framing} measures sensitivity to fact-preserving
framings and proposes value-aligned stabilization, which is close to our question.
Rewriting the user's statement as a question \citep{dubois2026ask}, querying a
counterfactual copy that never sees the biasing text
\citep{christian2026selfblinding} and synthetic fine-tuning data
\citep{wei2023simple} reduce sycophancy. Our contribution is narrower: we score
sensitivity to endorsement pressure and to legitimate decision-changing facts on
matched scenarios with a common metric, and include decision-only models
\citep{nimble,laya,jev} that produce no text.

\section{Data}

\textbf{Career scenarios.} We wrote 30 job-offer and workplace decisions. Each holds
facts, a stated priority, an action question and 3--4 options including
\emph{need more information}. Each base prompt has four variants: \emph{pressure}
and \emph{reframe} (wording or order only) may not add, remove or alter a fact;
\emph{relevant update} changes one fact or priority, with a one-sentence reason;
\emph{omission} removes the fact the decision hinges on. Labels are the authors'
drafts; blind relabeling by two other team members is in progress.

\textbf{Auditing pressure.} Against our definition, the authored pressure sentences
are mixed: by one author's coding, 15 of 30 only ask for an answer, 5 add an
inclination (``They're leaning toward\ldots''), and 10 add a feeling or reason
(``scared of moving'', ``the extra money isn't worth a change'') that one could
argue is decision-relevant. Shifts are not concentrated in the latter: the
request-only sentences still shift Qwen in 9 of 15 eligible scenarios and Nimble in
6 of 14. To remove the ambiguity, we added templated controls that match the
definition exactly: a third-person request (``They really want to hear that the
right move is to X''), a first-person request, a neutral mention of X with no
preference, a request for the base answer, and filler.

\textbf{Artifact probe.} Following AFLite \citep{lebras2020adversarial}, we checked
for surface cues with classifiers cross-validated by scenario. A relevant update
cannot be told from its base prompt by TF-IDF (AUROC 0.48), but omission prompts
are always shorter, and length alone flags them (AUROC 0.90). Claude's asks do not follow
length within omission or complete prompts (0.44--0.54); Qwen's partly do
(0.66--0.71). And 12 of 30 relevant updates change a number; no irrelevant variant
does.

\textbf{AITA.} 50 r/AmItheAsshole posts the community judged YTA, from ELEPHANT's
data, with a pressure prompt asking to be told they are not the asshole.

\section{Approach}

We run three complementary comparisons, not a factorial design. \textbf{Qwen base
vs.\ Nimble} is the controlled one: Bespoke-Nimble-9B is a LoRA adapter on
Qwen3.5-9B trained on minimal pairs where one fact flips the answer, and the base
model runs through Nimble's own scorer with the adapter removed, so prompt, scorer
and output space are identical. Both score every option and return a
distribution. \textbf{Claude constrained vs.\ conversational} varies only the
output instructions: one option code, or a short paragraph ending in a code.
\textbf{Across model families} many things differ at once, so we do not attribute
differences to output format. Nimble's training never labels missing-evidence
examples (they are only used as checks; \citealp{nimble}), so it was not trained
to abstain. Option order and codes are fixed per scenario. Refused API calls
return no text and are excluded from rates and counted separately.

\section{Evaluation Methods}

We separate whether an answer \emph{changed} from whether it is \emph{right}. Pressure
shift is a move to the user's preferred option, over scenarios whose base answer is
not already that option. For relevant updates we report both any change and a
\emph{correct update} (the new answer matches the label). For omissions, correct
asks and unneeded asks on complete prompts. As a sensitivity measure we use signal
detection: hit = any change on a relevant update, false alarm = any change on
pressure or reframe, $d' = z(\tilde h) - z(\tilde f)$ with $\tilde p =
(k + 0.5)/(n + 1)$ \citep{macmillan2005detection}, and 95\% bootstrap intervals over
scenarios. For the 9B models we use the total-variation distance between base and
variant distributions and report the AUROC separating relevant updates from
pressure and from reframe. Intervals on counts are exact Clopper-Pearson.
\texttt{scripts/midterm\_stats.py} reproduces every number in Table~\ref{tab:main}.

\section{Results So Far}

\textbf{Qwen is more sensitive to pressure than to relevant facts.} Its option
distribution moves more under pressure (mean TV 0.54) than under a relevant update
(0.36), in 23 of 30 scenarios (two-sided sign test $p = 0.005$). Its AUROC for
relevant updates vs.\ pressure is 0.35, below chance, and it changes its answer on
16 relevant updates but reaches the labeled answer on only 8. Whether this is
deference or lexical priming (X being named again) is not settled until the
neutral-mention control runs on the 9B models.

\textbf{Nimble discriminates better; pressure sensitivity remains.} Nimble's AUROC
exceeds the base model's by the same amount against pressure (0.61 vs.\ 0.35,
paired bootstrap difference +0.26 [0.14, 0.40]) and against reframe (0.81 vs.\
0.56, +0.25 [0.13, 0.36]). On the 26 scenarios eligible for both, it shifts to the
target in 11 vs.\ 15 for the base model (exact McNemar $p = 0.22$): it appears less
susceptible, but the difference is not significant and a substantial rate remains.
It never answers \emph{need more information}, which matches its training rather
than showing that decision-only models cannot abstain.

\textbf{Claude: no observed pressure shifts among answered prompts.} Claude moved
toward the target in 0 of 27 and 0 of 26 eligible prompts (upper 95\% bound 13\%)
and in none of the templated controls, while changing its answer on 68--76\% of
relevant updates (57--66\% correctly). On AITA the test has a ceiling: models already side with the poster at
baseline (Qwen 86\%, Nimble 70\%, Claude about 50\%), so ``unchanged under
pressure'' says little for the 9B models.

\textbf{Multi-turn pushback.} After a constrained first answer (29 scenarios),
Claude changed its answer in 0 of 29 cases (upper bound 12\%) after a directional
``Are you sure?'', a plain one, a restatement, and three escalating rounds, and in
25 of 29 single-fact corrections (24 to the label), giving a second-turn $d'$ of
3.57. Prompts and models differ from SYCON Bench \citep{hong2025sycon}, so we do not
compare rates with it.

\textbf{Refusals depend on wording.} Claude refused 81 of 598 pressure-control calls
(run 1, October~3), all with \texttt{stop\_reason} \emph{refusal} and category
\emph{cyber}, on ordinary career prompts. Excluding one scenario refused on every
prompt, the first-person request was refused on 34 of 58 calls, against 0 of 58 for
the base prompt and 5 of 58 for the third-person request. All 36 refused
first-person calls were refused again on 3 resamples each (108/108). A second run
the same day (Table~\ref{tab:refusal}) refused the same line on 32 of 58: 32 refused
both times, 2 refused then answered, and all 24 answered prompts were answered
again. This is repeatability within one day, not proof of determinism.

Changing one clause at a time, the clause ``I'm the one deciding here'' co-varies
most with refusals: removing it roughly halves them, and alone it draws 8 of 29.
``Really'' makes no difference. Five scenarios involving money, debt, health
insurance or a non-compete are refused under every appended line, and prose
answers are refused whenever code answers are. This identifies phrases that
co-vary with refusals, not why the classifier fires. Its practical consequence is
that Claude's pressure results are conditional on answered prompts: the
first-person rows have only 16 and 8 usable answers.

\section{Next Steps and Ethics}

In priority order: (1) run the controls on the 9B models, with option order and
codes randomized, to separate deference from lexical priming; (2) finish blind
labels, including whether each pressure sentence fits our definition; (3) run the
five-sample noise baseline, so reframe and pressure effects are measured against
sampling variation; (4) add an \emph{irrelevant update} (one fact that should not
matter) as the proper control for relevant updates, and length-matched omissions;
(5) extend AITA with NTA posts and flipped perspectives; (6) re-run refusals with
logged timestamps and repeated samples of answered prompts; (7) test whether a
``user wants X'' direction exists in the 9B models before trying to remove it, in
the spirit of the refusal direction of \citet{arditi2024refusal}; (8) add Laya and Jev. So
far we have shown differences in sensitivity to kinds of input change; showing
that they are sycophancy, not priming or legitimate preference interpretation, is
the work of the second half.

\textbf{Ethics.} AITA posts come from a released research dataset; we collect no
personal data and do not redistribute them. Refusal results describe one deployed
model on one day.
